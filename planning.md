# Provenance Guard: Planning Document

## Overview

Provenance Guard is a backend classification system that accepts text submissions, runs them through two independent detection signals, produces a confidence score representing how likely the content is AI-generated, and returns a transparency label a non-technical reader can understand. Creators can appeal any classification they disagree with. Every decision gets logged.

The core philosophy: we would rather say "we're not sure" than wrongly label a human's work as AI-generated. A false positive on a creative writing platform can damage a real person's reputation, so the system is designed to be conservative with high-confidence AI labels and generous with the benefit of the doubt.

## Detection Signals

### Signal 1: LLM-Based Classification (Groq)

**What it measures:** Semantic and stylistic coherence. The LLM reads the full text and evaluates whether the writing feels like it came from a language model or a human, picking up on things like generic phrasing, hedging patterns, overly balanced structure, and the kind of "says a lot without saying anything" quality that AI text often has.

**Output:** A float between 0.0 and 1.0, where 1.0 means "almost certainly AI-generated" and 0.0 means "almost certainly human." The LLM is prompted to return only a JSON object with a score and a short rationale.

**Blind spots:** LLMs are inconsistent judges. The same text can get different scores on different runs. The model also struggles with formal human writing (academic papers, legal text) because that style overlaps heavily with how AI writes. And if the submitted text was generated by the same model family doing the judging, there is a bias risk.

### Signal 2: Stylometric Heuristics (Pure Python)

**What it measures:** Statistical properties of the writing that tend to differ between human and AI text. Specifically:

- **Sentence length variance:** Human writers naturally vary their sentence lengths a lot. Some sentences are three words. Some run on for forty. AI text tends to cluster around a comfortable middle length. We measure the standard deviation of sentence lengths.
- **Type-token ratio (TTR):** Vocabulary diversity. Take the number of unique words divided by total words. AI text often reuses the same vocabulary more than humans do, especially in longer passages.
- **Punctuation density:** The ratio of punctuation marks to total characters. Human writing, especially casual or creative writing, uses more varied punctuation (dashes, ellipses, exclamation marks, parentheses). AI text is punctuation-conservative.

Each metric produces a sub-score between 0.0 and 1.0, and they are averaged into a single heuristic signal score. Higher means more likely AI.

**Output:** A float between 0.0 and 1.0, same direction as Signal 1.

**Blind spots:** Formal human writing (research papers, business reports) will score as AI-like because it naturally has low sentence length variance and conservative punctuation. Short texts (under 50 words) do not give the heuristics enough data to be reliable. Poetry with intentional repetition will also trip the TTR metric.

### Combining Signals

The two signals are combined using a weighted average:

```
combined_score = (0.6 * llm_score) + (0.4 * heuristic_score)
```

The LLM signal gets more weight because it captures semantic meaning, not just surface statistics. The heuristic signal acts as a structural cross-check. When both signals agree, confidence is higher. When they disagree, the combined score naturally lands in the uncertain range, which is exactly what we want.

## Uncertainty Representation

The combined score maps to three confidence tiers:

| Combined Score Range | Classification | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0.75 to 1.0 | High-confidence AI | Both signals strongly indicate AI-generated content |
| 0.40 to 0.74 | Uncertain | Signals are mixed or weak; system cannot make a confident call |
| 0.0 to 0.39 | High-confidence Human | Both signals strongly indicate human-written content |

A score of 0.6 means: "This text has some AI-like qualities, but we are not confident enough to label it as AI-generated." The system treats this as uncertain and tells the user exactly that.

The uncertain band is intentionally wide (0.40 to 0.74). This reflects the design choice that we would rather admit uncertainty than make a wrong call. Only when both signals clearly agree does the system commit to a high-confidence label.

## Transparency Label Design

Three label variants, written for a non-technical reader:

**High-confidence AI (score >= 0.75):**
> "Our analysis indicates this content is likely AI-generated (confidence: {score_percent}%). If you believe this is incorrect, you can submit an appeal."

**Uncertain (score 0.40 to 0.74):**
> "Our analysis was inconclusive for this content (confidence: {score_percent}%). The result may reflect either AI-assisted or human-written text. If you believe this result is wrong, you can submit an appeal."

**High-confidence Human (score < 0.40):**
> "Our analysis indicates this content is likely human-written (confidence: {score_percent}%). No action is needed."

Notes on label design:
- Confidence percentage in the label is calculated as: for AI classification, it is the raw combined score as a percentage. For human classification, it is (1 minus the combined score) as a percentage. This way, the percentage always represents confidence in the stated classification, not in a fixed direction.
- The word "likely" appears in every label because the system never claims certainty.
- The appeal option is mentioned in AI and uncertain labels but not in the human label, since a human classification is unlikely to be contested by the creator.

## Appeals Workflow

**Who can appeal:** Any creator whose content has been classified. They submit an appeal using the `content_id` from the original classification response.

**What they provide:**
- `content_id` (required): identifies which classification they are contesting
- `creator_reasoning` (required): a free-text explanation of why they believe the classification is wrong

**What the system does on appeal:**
1. Looks up the original classification by `content_id`
2. Updates the content status from `"classified"` to `"under_review"`
3. Writes a new audit log entry recording the appeal, including the creator's reasoning and a timestamp
4. Returns a confirmation response with the updated status

**What a human reviewer would see:** The audit log entry for an appealed item includes the original classification (attribution, confidence, both signal scores) alongside the creator's reasoning. A reviewer can compare the system's evidence against the creator's explanation and make a judgment call. Automated re-classification is not part of this system.

## Anticipated Edge Cases

**1. Academic or formal human writing scored as AI.**
A human-written research abstract or policy brief will have low sentence length variance, conservative punctuation, and formal vocabulary. The heuristic signal will flag it as AI-like. The LLM signal may also lean AI because formal writing overlaps with LLM output style. Result: a genuine human writer gets a high AI score. This is the exact false positive scenario the wide uncertainty band is meant to catch, but very formal writing could still land above 0.75. The appeals workflow exists specifically for this case.

**2. Lightly edited AI output that looks casual.**
If someone generates text with AI and then manually adds typos, casual phrasing, and varied punctuation, the heuristic signal will score it as human-like (high variance, informal punctuation). The LLM signal may or may not catch the underlying structure. Result: the system could undercount AI involvement. This is a known limitation of any text-based detection approach.

**3. Very short submissions (under 50 words).**
Short text does not give the heuristic signal enough data. Sentence length variance on 2 to 3 sentences is essentially noise. The system will lean heavily on the LLM signal alone, which makes the confidence score less reliable. These submissions will often land in the uncertain band, which is actually the correct behavior for low-information inputs.

## Architecture

```
Submission Flow:
POST /submit (text, creator_id)
       |
       v
 [Input Validation]
       |
       +----------+-----------+
       |                      |
       v                      v
 [Signal 1: Groq LLM]  [Signal 2: Stylometrics]
  (semantic score)       (structural score)
       |                      |
       +----------+-----------+
                  |
                  v
         [Confidence Scoring]
      (weighted average: 0.6/0.4)
                  |
                  v
        [Label Generation]
      (map score to label tier)
                  |
                  v
          [Audit Log Write]
                  |
                  v
        [JSON Response returned]


Appeal Flow:
POST /appeal (content_id, creator_reasoning)
       |
       v
 [Lookup original classification]
       |
       v
 [Update status -> "under_review"]
       |
       v
 [Audit Log Write (appeal entry)]
       |
       v
 [JSON Response returned]
```

**Submission flow in words:** A creator submits text via POST /submit. The system validates the input, then runs it through two independent detection signals in sequence. The Groq LLM evaluates semantic patterns, the stylometric function evaluates structural statistics. Both scores feed into a weighted average to produce a combined confidence score. That score is mapped to one of three transparency label tiers. The full result (scores, label, attribution) is written to the audit log, and the JSON response goes back to the creator.

**Appeal flow in words:** A creator submits an appeal via POST /appeal with their content_id and reasoning. The system finds the original classification, flips the status to "under_review," logs the appeal alongside the original decision, and returns a confirmation.
